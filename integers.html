    Section 3.4 Integers

    Objectives: Learning Objectives

    1. Describe how the integers extend the whole numbers, and represent integers on a number line.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use number lines and counters to explain integer addition and subtraction.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Explain subtraction as adding an additive inverse.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Use properties of arithmetic to explain and perform integer multiplication and division.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Prove that \((-1)a=-a\) and \((-1)(-1)=1\text{,}\) and use these results to explain the multiplication sign rules.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Interpret integer calculations in problems involving debts, changes in position, and losses.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.1 Why Do We Need Negative Numbers?

    So far, we have mostly worked with whole numbers. We can add or multiply any two whole numbers and obtain another whole number, but subtraction gives us a problem. What should \(3-5\) mean? Using our relationship between subtraction and addition, we are looking for a number \(x\) such that \(x+5=3\text{.}\) There is no whole number that does this. So, if we want to be able to subtract any two whole numbers, we need to extend our number system.
    🔗
    You have probably used negative numbers before, perhaps when talking about a temperature below zero or an amount of money owed. Let’s think about what these numbers mean and how their arithmetic fits with the properties we have already discussed.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.1.

    The integers consist of the whole numbers and the negatives of the positive whole numbers. We write this set as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can represent these numbers by extending our whole-number line to the left of zero, keeping the same spacing between consecutive integers. One unit to the left of zero is \(-1\text{,}\) two units to the left is \(-2\text{,}\) and so on. Numbers farther to the right are greater. For example, \(-2>-5\) because \(-2\) is to the right of \(-5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Remember our definition of an additive inverse: two numbers are additive inverses if their sum is zero. The numbers \(5\) and \(-5\) are additive inverses, since \(5+(-5)=0\text{.}\) On the number line, they are the same distance from zero but on opposite sides. We also call them opposites.
    🔗
    Be careful with the notation \(-a\text{.}\) This means the opposite of \(a\text{;}\) it does not necessarily mean a negative number. For example, the opposite of \(-5\) is \(5\text{,}\) so \(-(-5)=5\text{.}\) The opposite of zero is zero. In general, taking the opposite twice brings us back to our original number: \(-(-a)=a\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.2.

    The absolute value of an integer \(a\text{,}\) written \(|a|\text{,}\) is its distance from zero on the number line.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, \(|5|=5\text{,}\) \(|-5|=5\text{,}\) and \(|0|=0\text{.}\) Note that absolute value tells us the distance from zero, but not which side of zero the number lies on. This is why comparing absolute values alone does not tell us which integer is greater.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.2 Integer Addition

    Adding on a Number Line.

    Let’s start with \(3+5\text{.}\) On a number line, we can begin at \(3\) and move five units to the right, ending at \(8\text{.}\) To include negative numbers, we use the sign of the number being added to tell us the direction: adding a positive number moves us to the right, and adding a negative number moves us to the left.
    🔗
    For example, to calculate \(3+(-5)\text{,}\) we start at \(3\) and move five units left. The first three units take us to zero, and the remaining two take us to \(-2\text{.}\) Thus \(3+(-5)=-2\text{.}\) If we instead start at \(-3\) and move five units left, we end at \(-8\text{,}\) so \(-3+(-5)=-8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.3.

    Calculate \(-7+4\text{.}\) How can we explain the answer using counters?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Suppose a counter marked \(+\) represents \(1\text{,}\) and a counter marked \(-\) represents \(-1\text{.}\) One of each has a total value of zero, so we call them a zero pair. Adding or removing a zero pair does not change the value represented by our counters.
    🔗
    We begin with seven negative counters and add four positive counters. We can now remove four zero pairs, leaving three negative counters. Thus \(-7+4=-3\text{.}\) On the number line, the same calculation starts at \(-7\) and moves four units right, ending at \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    What Do the Models Tell Us?

    We can use these models to describe a procedure for adding integers. When both numbers are positive, all of the counters are positive, and when both are negative, all of the counters are negative. In either case, we add their absolute values and keep their common sign.
    🔗
    When one number is positive and the other is negative, we remove zero pairs. The number of counters left is the difference between the absolute values, and their sign is the sign of the number with the larger absolute value. What if the absolute values are equal? Then every counter belongs to a zero pair, and the sum is zero. Adding zero leaves the original number unchanged.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.4.

    Maria has no money and owes 15 dollars. She earns 20 dollars and uses some of it to repay the debt. How much money does she have left?
    🔗
    Solution.
    If we represent money available by positive numbers and money owed by negative numbers, Maria begins with a balance of \(-15\) dollars. Her earnings increase this balance by \(20\) dollars, giving
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -15+20=5. \end{equation*}
    Fifteen dollars of her earnings cancel the debt, leaving her with 5 dollars. This is the same idea as removing fifteen zero pairs.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.5.

    Use either a number line or counters to explain \(4+(-7)\text{,}\) \(-4+(-7)\text{,}\) and \(-4+4\text{.}\) For each calculation, explain why the answer is positive, negative, or zero.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.3 Properties of Integer Addition

    The properties of addition that we used for whole numbers also hold for integers. Let’s collect them here, paying particular attention to what changes when negative numbers are available. For any integers \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{,}\) we have:
    🔗
    1. Commutativity: \(a+b=b+a\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Associativity: \((a+b)+c=a+(b+c)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Closure: \(a+b\) is an integer.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Additive identity: \(a+0=0+a=a\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Additive inverse: \(a+(-a)=(-a)+a=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The last property is the new possibility here: every integer has an additive inverse within the integers. This was not true in the whole numbers. For example, there is no whole number we can add to \(7\) to obtain zero, but the integer \(-7\) does exactly this.
    🔗
    These properties can make calculations easier. Suppose we want to add \(-17+8+17\text{.}\) We can rearrange and regroup to obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -17+8+17=(-17+17)+8=0+8=8. \end{equation*}
    Here we used commutativity and associativity to bring the opposites together, then the additive inverse and additive identity properties to finish the calculation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.4 Integer Subtraction

    Subtraction and Addition.

    Earlier, we described subtraction using a related addition problem. For example, \(8-3=5\) because \(5+3=8\text{.}\) We want to keep this relationship when we include negative numbers. So, to calculate \(a-b\text{,}\) we look for a number \(x\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+b=a. \end{equation*}
    Since \(b\) now has an additive inverse, we can add \(-b\) to both sides:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (x+b)+(-b)=a+(-b). \end{equation*}
    Using associativity, the additive inverse property, and the additive identity property, the left-hand side becomes \(x+(b+(-b))=x+0=x\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-b=a+(-b). \end{equation*}
    This explains the rule you may already know: to subtract a number, add its opposite. In particular, \(5-(-3)=5+3=8\) because the opposite of \(-3\) is \(3\text{.}\) We can check this using the original meaning of subtraction: \(8+(-3)=5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Removing Counters.

    How can we see \(5-(-3)\) using counters? Begin with five positive counters. We want to remove three negative counters, but we do not have any! Remember, however, that we can add zero pairs without changing the value. Add three zero pairs. We now have eight positive counters and three negative counters, still representing \(5\text{.}\) Removing the three negative counters leaves eight positive counters, so \(5-(-3)=8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On a number line, rewriting subtraction as addition tells us which way to move. Subtracting a positive number moves us left, while subtracting a negative number moves us right. The direction depends on the number being subtracted, not on whether our starting point is positive or negative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.6.

    Calculate \(-10-(-7)\text{,}\) and check the answer using addition.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The opposite of \(-7\) is \(7\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -10-(-7)=-10+7=-3. \end{equation*}
    To check, we add the subtracted number back to the answer: \(-3+(-7)=-10\text{.}\) Notice that subtracting a negative number increased our starting value, but the answer is still negative.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.7.

    Alex owes his friend 8 dollars and repays 3 dollars. Represent the change in his debt using integers.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let’s record money owed as a negative number. The debt begins at \(-8\) dollars. Repaying 3 dollars removes 3 dollars of debt, represented by \(-3\text{,}\) so the remaining debt is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -8-(-3)=-8+3=-5. \end{equation*}
    Alex still owes 5 dollars. We are tracking the debt here; we are not calculating how much cash he has left after making the payment.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Properties of Subtraction.

    The integers are closed under subtraction: subtracting any integer from another gives an integer. We can now calculate \(3-5=-2\text{,}\) which was not possible within the whole numbers.
    🔗
    This follows from writing \(a-b=a+(-b)\text{:}\) both \(a\) and \(-b\) are integers, and the integers are closed under addition.
    🔗
    Subtraction is still neither commutative nor associative. For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3-5=-2,\qquad 5-3=2, \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (3-5)-2=-4,\qquad 3-(5-2)=0. \end{equation*}
    So we must keep track of both the order of the numbers and the placement of brackets.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.5 Integer Multiplication

    We know how to multiply whole numbers, but what should happen when one or both factors are negative? We want multiplication on the integers to agree with whole-number multiplication and to retain the commutative, associative, and distributive properties, with \(1\) as the multiplicative identity. Let’s see what these requirements tell us about the signs of products. Multiplication by zero still gives zero; the argument from the preceding section uses these same properties.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.1 Starting with Repeated Addition

    We can interpret \(3\times(-5)\) as three groups of \(-5\text{.}\) Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\times(-5)=(-5)+(-5)+(-5)=-15. \end{equation*}
    Using negative counters, this gives three groups of five negative counters. On the number line, we can start at zero and make three moves of five units to the left. Both models give \(-15\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 3.4.8.

    Calculate \((-6)\times4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Commutativity lets us write this as \(4\times(-6)\text{,}\) which we can interpret as four groups of \(-6\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-6)\times4=4\times(-6)=(-6)+(-6)+(-6)+(-6)=-24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.9.

    A submarine starts at the surface and descends 30 metres each minute for 5 minutes. Where is it relative to the surface?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let the surface have position zero, with positions below the surface represented by negative numbers. Each minute changes the submarine’s position by \(-30\) metres. After five minutes its position is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5\times(-30)=-150. \end{equation*}
    So the submarine is 150 metres below the surface. The signed position is \(-150\) metres; its depth below the surface is 150 metres.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Repeated addition helps when one factor tells us a whole number of groups. But what would a negative number of groups mean? To explain products of two negative numbers, let’s use the arithmetic properties we want to preserve.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.2 Multiplying by Negative One

    We have used \(-a\) to mean the additive inverse of \(a\text{.}\) Does this give the same result as multiplying \(a\) by \(-1\text{?}\) We should show why these expressions are equal before using them interchangeably.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.4.10.

    For every integer \(a\text{,}\) \((-1)\times a=-a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We know that \(1+(-1)=0\text{.}\) Multiplying this equality by \(a\) and distributing gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (1+(-1))\times a=0\times a, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 1\times a+(-1)\times a=0. \end{equation*}
    Since \(1\times a=a\text{,}\) we have \(a+(-1)\times a=0\text{.}\) Thus \((-1)\times a\) is the additive inverse of \(a\text{,}\) which is \(-a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The additive inverse is unique: if \(a+x=0\text{,}\) adding \(-a\) to both sides gives \(x=-a\text{.}\) So identifying a number whose sum with \(a\) is zero really does determine that number.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.3 Why Is a Negative Times a Negative Positive?

    Let’s first consider \((-1)\times(-1)\text{.}\) The previous result already tells us that this is the opposite of \(-1\text{,}\) namely \(1\text{.}\) We can also see directly why distributivity requires this answer.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.4.11.

    \((-1)\times(-1)=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since \(1+(-1)=0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-1)\times(1+(-1))=(-1)\times0=0. \end{equation*}
    Distributing and using the multiplicative identity gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -1+(-1)\times(-1)=0. \end{equation*}
    Adding \(1\) to both sides leaves \((-1)\times(-1)=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is the same kind of reasoning we used to explain multiplication by zero. If we want these arithmetic properties to continue to hold, the value of \((-1)\times(-1)\) must be \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let \(a\) and \(b\) be positive integers. We can use our two results, together with associativity and commutativity, to obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-a)(-b)=\big((-1)a\big)\big((-1)b\big)=\big((-1)(-1)\big)(ab)=ab. \end{equation*}
    Thus multiplying two negative integers gives a positive integer. For example, \((-3)(-5)=3\times5=15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.4 Using the Multiplication Rules

    We can now collect the rules we have explained. Multiply the absolute values to find the absolute value of the product. If both factors are nonzero, equal signs give a positive product and different signs give a negative product. If either factor is zero, the product is zero.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.12.

    Calculate \((-4)(-7)\text{,}\) \(4(-7)\text{,}\) and \((-4)0\text{.}\) Then explain why \((-a)b=-(ab)\) for positive integers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) using \(-a=(-1)a\) and associativity.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.6 Properties of Integer Multiplication

    The properties we used above hold for all integers, including zero. For integers \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{,}\) we have:
    🔗
    1. Commutativity: \(ab=ba\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Associativity: \((ab)c=a(bc)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Distributivity: \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Closure: \(ab\) is an integer.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Multiplicative identity: \(a\times1=1\times a=a\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    For example, we can use distribution to calculate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-7)\times19=(-7)\times(20+(-1))=-140+7=-133. \end{equation*}
    Notice that we used the product of two negative numbers in the second term. The same properties that explain the sign rules also give us ways to simplify calculations.
    🔗
    What about multiplicative inverses? Every integer has an additive inverse in \(\mathbb{Z}\text{,}\) but most integers do not have a multiplicative inverse in \(\mathbb{Z}\text{.}\) For example, there is no integer \(x\) such that \(2x=1\text{.}\) The only integers with integer multiplicative inverses are \(1\) and \(-1\text{,}\) each of which is its own inverse. This leads us to the remaining limitation of integer arithmetic: division.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.7 Integer Division

    Using a Related Multiplication Problem.

    Let’s use the relationship between multiplication and division to work out the sign rules. For now, we are looking for an exact quotient: if \(b\neq0\text{,}\) then \(a\div b=q\) means that \(bq=a\text{.}\) This is the zero-remainder case of our earlier work on division.
    🔗

    Example 3.4.13.

    Calculate \(-20\div4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We are looking for a number \(q\) such that \(4q=-20\text{.}\) Since \(4\times(-5)=-20\text{,}\) we have \(-20\div4=-5\text{.}\) The multiplication equation also checks our answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    What if both numbers are negative? To calculate \(-20\div(-4)\text{,}\) we look for \(q\) such that \((-4)q=-20\text{.}\) Here \(q=5\text{.}\) Similarly, \(20\div(-4)=-5\) because \((-4)(-5)=20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So division has the same sign rules as multiplication: for nonzero dividends and divisors, equal signs give a positive quotient and different signs give a negative quotient. Also, \(0\div b=0\) whenever \(b\neq0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Interpreting an Integer Quotient.

    We can see \(-20\div4=-5\) by sharing twenty negative counters equally among four groups. Each group contains five negative counters. For a negative divisor, it is clearer to return to the related multiplication equation than to talk about a negative number of groups. A model is useful when we can explain what its numbers and operations represent.
    🔗

    Example 3.4.14.

    A company has a total loss of 60,000 dollars over 12 months. What is its average monthly loss?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Representing losses by negative numbers, the average monthly change is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -60{,}000\div12=-5{,}000. \end{equation*}
    The average monthly loss is 5,000 dollars. This does not mean the company lost exactly that amount every month; it is the amount that would give the same total if the loss were spread equally over the twelve months.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Division can still take us outside the integers. For example, no integer \(q\) satisfies \(4q=7\text{,}\) so \(7\div4\) has no integer exact quotient. We can describe it using a quotient and remainder, \(7=4\times1+3\text{,}\) or use the rational number \(7/4\text{.}\) We will study rational numbers later. Thus the integers are closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication, but not under division, even when we exclude division by zero.
    🔗
    Division by zero is still undefined. If \(a\neq0\text{,}\) there is no number \(q\) satisfying \(0q=a\text{.}\) If \(a=0\text{,}\) every integer \(q\) satisfies \(0q=0\text{,}\) so there is no unique quotient. Neither case gives us a well-defined division operation.
    🔗
