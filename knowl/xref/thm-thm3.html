Theorem 7.1.13.

If a number \(a\) is divisible by \(c\) but a number \(b\) is not divisible by \(c\text{,}\) then \(a+b\) and \(a-b\) are not divisible by \(c\text{.}\)
🔗
🔗

Proof.
Again, we do the additive case, and leave the subtraction case to an exercise later. Let \(c \mid a\) and \(c \nmid b\text{.}\) This means that \(a=cx\) for some natural number \(x\text{,}\) and \(b=cy+r\text{,}\) where \(y\) is a natural number, and thinking about it as a remainder, \(r\) is a natural number such that \(r \leq c-1\text{.}\) We need to show that \(c \nmid a+b\text{,}\) so we need to show that \(a+b\) has a remainder. Adding both equations together, we get
\begin{equation*} a+b = cx+cy+r. \end{equation*}
Factoring out an \(c\) from the first two terms on the RHS,
\begin{equation*} a+b = c(x+y)+r. \end{equation*}
Since \(x+y \in \mathbb{N}\) and \(r \in \mathbb{N}\) with \(1 \leq r \leq c-1\text{,}\) we have written \(a+b\) in quotient-remainder form, and we know there is exactly one way to do this. And since \(r \neq 0\text{,}\) then \(c \nmid a+b\text{.}\)
